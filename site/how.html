How it works

Every prediction comes from three steps: rate everyone from past results, turn two ratings into match chances, then play out the rest of the competition thousands of times.

1. Rate every team and player

Each soccer club, national team and tennis player has an Elo rating: one number that goes up when they win and down when they lose. Beating a stronger opponent earns more than beating a weaker one, so after a few seasons the numbers settle where results put them.

  • Club soccer uses every match since 2021 in the big five leagues, the Champions, Europa and Conference Leagues, and five more leagues (Portugal, Netherlands, Scotland, Belgium, Turkey), so clubs from different countries are measured on one scale. Wins by bigger margins count for more, and home teams get a head start that the model fits from the data.
  • International soccer uses every international since 1990; World Cup and continental tournament matches count more than friendlies.
  • Tennis uses every ATP and WTA singles match since 2024. New players' ratings move fast and established players' slowly (the approach FiveThirtyEight popularised). Grand Slam matches count a little more. Each player also has a rating per surface (hard, clay, grass) built only from matches on that surface. A prediction blends the two; the mix is fitted to the last year of results .

2. Turn two ratings into match chances

Soccer: the rating gap (plus home advantage) sets how many goals each side should score, for example 1.9 against 0.9. Goals are treated as random (Poisson) draws around those averages. That one model gives the home win / draw / away win chances, the most likely score and, by adding the chances up, everything else.

Tennis: the classic Elo formula gives the chance one player beats another over three sets. For men's Grand Slams (best of five) it's converted through the implied chance of winning each set, which makes favourites a bit safer over five sets.

3. Play the rest out 10,000 times

To pick a winner the model doesn't just pick the best-rated side. It simulates every remaining match 10,000 times, keeping real results already played:

  • Leagues (Premier League, La Liga…): finish the table, break ties on goal difference and goals scored, see who's top.
  • Champions and Europa League: finish the 36-team league phase, then the knockout playoffs (9th–24th), round of 16 and onwards, with two-legged ties and a one-off final.
  • International tournaments: finish the groups, then the knockout bracket.
  • World Cup qualifying: finish each group table and count how often a team lands in a qualifying place, a playoff place, or top spot, using each confederation's places (for example, in Europe the winner qualifies and the runner-up goes to the playoffs; in South America the top six qualify). These pages appear on their own while qualifiers are being played; 2030 qualifying hadn't been scheduled as of October 2026.
  • Tennis: play the rest of the draw round by round.
  • ATP and WTA Finals: two round-robin groups of four (most wins first, a two-way tie goes to head-to-head), then semi-finals (group winner against the other group's runner-up) and a final. Before the groups are drawn, the field is projected from the current top 8 in the rankings and the draw is simulated too.

A team's title chance is simply how often it won across those 10,000 runs.

4. Odds over time

The charts replay the season: for each match day the model uses only the results and ratings known at the end of that day, then runs the simulation again. That's how the line can be drawn from before the first match, and it shows how the race changed as results came in. Today's point moves with every update (about hourly), and a new day adds a new point.

The charts also show dots: the odds this site actually published on each day. The replay can shift a little as ratings settle or the model improves; the dots never change, so you can see how the live calls compared with hindsight.

5. How accurate is it?

Each match is checked against the prediction the model would have made just before kick-off, over the last 12 months:

A "called it" means the outcome we rated most likely happened. Soccer has three outcomes (and draws are hard to call), so around 50% is respectable; tennis has two, so it scores higher. The Brier score measures how close the probabilities were (0 is perfect; lower is better).

Do the percentages mean what they say?

Being right often isn't enough: when the model says 70%, that should happen about 70% of the time. The chart groups every prediction from the last 12 months into 10% bands and plots how often those things actually happened. Points on the dashed line are spot on. If the high bands sit below the line and the low bands above it, the model was too confident.

6. Where the data comes from

  • Fixtures, results and tennis draws: ESPN's public scoreboard feeds.
  • International results since 1872: martj42/international_results.

7. What it doesn't know

  • Injuries, suspensions, rotation, transfers and managers: only results go in.
  • Tennis surfaces come from a list of tournament names (ESPN doesn't publish them); unlisted events count as hard court, and indoor and outdoor hard courts are treated alike.
  • ESPN doesn't publish draw positions, so open slots in a tennis bracket are filled from the right round but not always the exact half of the draw. UEFA knockout draws are approximated by seeding.
  • Points deductions and soccer head-to-head tie-breakers aren't modelled. At the tennis Finals, three-way ties are split at random rather than by sets and games won.
  • World Cup qualifying places follow the 2026 cycle until FIFA confirms 2030's, and "best runners-up" rules (Africa, North & Central America) are simplified to "every runner-up".
  • The projected Finals field uses the 52-week rankings because ESPN doesn't publish the Race; withdrawals and alternates only show up once the real draw does.
